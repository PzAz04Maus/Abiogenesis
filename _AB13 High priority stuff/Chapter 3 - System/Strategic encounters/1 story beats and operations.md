# Version 2

Similar to the traditions of books, stage, or cinema, the  roleplaying game presents the dramatic beat of scenes and sequels as an encounter. The encounter typically consists of a single, contiguous point in time, such as a conversation, an affair, or a battle of sword and muscle.

For fantasy, this understanding is enough because any counterexamples are treated as deviations from the norm. But the modern story spans nonlinearly across vast regions of time and space. In fiction, examples include car chases, travelling, battles, being hunted down, a heist, or contact.

In real life, an example would be the Vietnamese People's Army (PAVN) doctrine of "one slow, four quick:" Slow plan, quick advance, quick attack, quick clearance and quick withdrawal. In this case the planning phase is the slowest, the attack is the quickest, and the advance and withdrawal phases are in-between.

# the [[strategic story]] in play

In consequence to the growth of the Industrial Age, there has been a non-linear increase in sophistication. As wealth and the efficiency to use it grows, the machinations and objectives of their owners also elaborate.  The story of the knight, the marshall, and the landholder thus gives way to the story of the detective, the policeman, the soldier, and city hall. And just as such complexity arises in real life, so too must be in its deep imitation.

To manage this complexity for story, we can start by using one of the most well developed arts available: war. The theorist Carl von Clausewitz distinguishes the art of war into two activities: its conduct (tactics) and their arrangement in pursuit of an objective (strategy). 
Storytelling has some theory which maps to these activities (scenes, conflicts, goals), but the advantage of Clausewitz's approach lies in situations where the story is interactive. Because unlike traditional forms:

1. the result of the interactive story can be uncertain, making it a game
2. the audience can decide how to manage the story by arranging their approach. Their decisions for when and how to fight can have a massive impact on the outcome - note: traditional story is linear, war is nonlinear

We can therefore grant the players extra discretion by increasing the range of decisions possible, which puts tabletop stories about complex phenomena (such as contemporary stories) in a unique position.


# levels for the individual

Borrowing from military theory, we can characterize player reasoning on three levels: tactical, operational, and strategic. 

The main difference between military theory and the rpg characterization of it is that the level of reasoning is not dependent on the level of forces commanded, but on the type and pace of reasoning. For example, assassinating the king may be a tactical or operational decision, but the players reasoning for why it's necessary would be strategic.

Tabletop strategy however, is further differentiated because Players are not always mandated to think on the other scales to "win." The gm may take on some of the higher responsibilities in the interest of dramatic pacing or because the players don't have the knowledge or inclination to think on this level. Unlike War, not all "victories" in rpgs are dependent on the protagonists combat success, but on enjoyment. Unlile in war, tragedy holds as much value as fortune in the story. 

Despite this distinction, these levels of analysis can still be useful for the gm as a complement to the narrative needs of the story: the climax of the story can be related to the resolution of the main strategic objective, the parts of the rising action or the sequences can be linked to the operational scale, and the individual scenes are in the tactical layer.


In the rpg, the martial levels are concerned about the scale of the decisions made and the reasons for them, independent of group size or value.

In times of direct combat, decisions may occur seconds apart. At this level, leaders rely on what Nobel Prize winner Daniel Kahneman described as System 1 for their decisions—using heuristics, instinct, and intuition to make decisions rapidly.

From the division through combatant command levels, the pace of decisions occurs over a wider range of time. It is at the operational level of warfare where leaders begin to rely most heavily on Kahneman’s System 2, where an individual must pay attention to his or her decision-making process



# mismatches in Distance and time
[[Mismatch criticism]]
Lets make some basic observations. The definition of speed is distance over time. This anodyne observation has major ramifications for drama, since the speed of a game has an impact on the story and the players. For story, speed determines how hard the crash of drama will be, and for the players, the number of time increments has an impact on player engagement.

But we just can't make the distance units as big as possible and the time increments as small as possible. Game Settings exist in a context, and players have can make only so many decisions before the game exhausts them. Worse, static time increments aren't universally appropriate - a 400 mile road trip doesn't need 6 second turns, but a barfight might. These mismatches have a cost to the value of the game.

For dungeons and dragons, this issue of opposing increments isn't as bad because the probability of a mismatch between the players and the environment is low; even dragons are a low probability event, while typical deviations exist in the form of mounts, sailing ships, and magic. So mismatches were rare, and when they did occur they usually didn't need much intervention.

This is not so in a setting with the car, gun and radio. In modern-esque settings, these mismatches are incredibly varied and numerous: it's rare to be attacked by a dragon, but it's a statistic to be hit by a car.

In most modern games, the solution has been to kludge a solution out of the design language of fantasy games or raw storytelling. This solution is probably also why Hollywood movies are known as a trope for their poor imitation of firearms. 

While this has the great advantage of keeping games brisk and interesting,  the flaw is that it leaves out the ability to explore the interactions in and between these different scales. ~~Dnd cannot run a car chase very well out of the box.~~
