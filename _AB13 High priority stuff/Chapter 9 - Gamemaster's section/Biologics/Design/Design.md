# Bipedalism

## Vertical biped
Orthograde bipedalism, where the Torso held vertically over hips.

### advantages

Hands freed for manipulation – key in primates and especially humans (tool use, carrying, throwing).

Height advantage – seeing over tall grass, scanning for predators or prey.

Efficient long-distance walking – the human striding gait with heel-to-toe rolling conserves energy for endurance travel.

Breathing while moving – upright posture decouples locomotion from respiration (important for persistence hunting in humans).

Trade-offs: Orthograde posture reduces sprint speed potential and puts stress on the spine and knees.

## Horizontal biped
Pronograde bipedalism, where the torso is held more or less horizontal to the ground during normal locomotion.

Also called forward leaning

Torso pitched forward, balance maintained by tail or other support. Tail can be counterbalance or prop

Speed and stability – horizontal posture keeps the center of mass directly above or slightly ahead of the hips, allowing powerful hindlimb propulsion.

Aerodynamics – streamlined body posture for running (e.g., ostriches, theropods).

Power storage – kangaroos use their tendons and pronograde alignment for spring-loaded hopping efficiency.

Trade-offs: Limits hand use (forelimbs are specialized for other functions), and requires long tails or other structures to maintain balance.

## Transitional / Facultative Bipeds

Animals that are normally quadrupeds but can shift into bipedal stance or locomotion.

Bears – orthograde stance (for short bouts, not true locomotion).

Chimps, bonobos, gorillas – facultative orthograde bipeds.

Some lizards – facultative pronograde bipeds when sprinting.

## Notes: couldn't a kangaroo have its hands freed for manipulation

Locomotion: During hopping, the forelimbs aren’t needed — propulsion comes entirely from the hind legs and tail balance. So in theory, the arms are “free.”

At rest / slow movement: Kangaroos use a unique “pentapedal gait,” where they lean forward and use their tail as a fifth limb while the forelimbs help push the body forward. That means at low speeds their arms aren’t free — they’re part of walking.

Function: Kangaroo arms are adapted for grasping vegetation, grooming, and fighting (male kangaroos “box” with them). They can manipulate, but their range and dexterity are limited.

Trunk orientation (pronograde) – In a horizontal body plan, the shoulders are pitched forward. The arms naturally align under or ahead of the torso, so they’re biomechanically recruited for balance/support in slow gaits.

Hand anatomy – Kangaroo hands are not as flexible as primate hands (reduced thumb, stiff digits). They can grip branches or food but not fine-manipulate tools.

Locomotion compromise – Because they rely on the forelimbs during pentapedal walking, their arms never became “fully emancipated” like ours.
