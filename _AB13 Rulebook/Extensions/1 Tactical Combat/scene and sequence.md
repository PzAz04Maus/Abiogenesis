## **17 Best Tips for Writing a Scene With Multiple Locations**

The simple act of moving from one location to another can unfold layers of a character’s story, revealing hidden depths.

Let’s go over the best tips for how to write a scene with multiple locations.

### **1. The Eiffel Elevator – Establish a Central Anchor Point**

Start by establishing a ‘home base’ location, like the Eiffel Tower in a whirlwind tour of Paris.

This anchor point grounds your readers, giving them a reference point to return to as the scene shifts.

**It’s crucial in maintaining continuity and avoiding disorientation.**

**Example:** Imagine a story set in a magical version of Paris. Your protagonist starts at a café near the Eiffel Tower, then whisks away to a sorcerer’s shop in Montmartre, but references the Tower’s view at each location, creating a cohesive thread through the scenes.

### **2. The Sahara Shift – Use Environmental Transitions**

Transition between locations by focusing on the environment.

Like stepping from the scorching Sahara to a cool oasis, use sensory details to contrast settings.

This method creates a smooth flow, making the location change feel natural and easy to follow.

**Example:** Your character might leave the bustling, noisy city streets, stepping into the tranquility of a secluded park. Describe the sudden hush, the rustle of leaves, and the scent of grass, transporting the reader smoothly to this new setting.

### **3. The Venetian Vignette – Frame Each Location Uniquely**

**Treat each location as a distinct vignette, like a unique scene in a Venetian play.**

This approach involves focusing on what makes each location special.

Highlight its unique characteristics to give every setting its moment in the spotlight.

**Example:** In a spy thriller, the protagonist might dash from a rainy London street to a sunny Roman piazza. Each location is painted vividly – the gloom and dampness of London contrasted with Rome’s warmth and the aroma of fresh espresso.

### **4. The Amazonian Arrow – Create a Directional Flow**

Ensure your scene flows in a clear direction, like following the course of the Amazon River.

**Avoid jumping back and forth randomly between locations. Instead, create a logical sequence that guides the reader smoothly from one place to the next.**

**Example:** In an adventure novel, the hero might start in a bustling marketplace, move through narrow alleyways, and finally arrive at a hidden temple. The progression feels natural, like following a treasure map.

### **5. The Himalayan Harmony – Balance Descriptions Evenly**

Like finding equilibrium on a Himalayan peak, balance the level of detail across locations.

Don’t lavish too much description on one and skimp on another.

This balance helps maintain pacing and keeps the reader equally engaged with each setting.

**Example:** If your scene moves from a detailed, vibrant carnival to a stark, quiet library, give each the same level of descriptive attention. Describe the carnival’s colors and sounds, then the library’s solemnity and the whisper of turning pages.

### **6. The Polynesian Path – Link Locations Thematically**

Connect your locations with a thematic thread, like islands linked by a Polynesian path.

**This approach involves finding a common theme or element in each location and weaving it through the narrative.**

It adds depth and creates a more cohesive experience for the reader.

**Example:** In a story exploring themes of freedom, each location can reflect this concept differently. A bustling city market represents economic freedom, a remote mountain village illustrates freedom from modern constraints, and a seaside town shows freedom through travel and exploration.

### **7. The Andean Altitude – Elevate Emotions with Each Location**

Just like climbing the Andes, elevate emotions with each new setting.

Use the unique atmosphere of each location to intensify your character’s feelings and experiences.

This method ensures that the location changes contribute to character development.

**Example: A character might start feeling claustrophobic and anxious in a crowded urban setting, then become contemplative and serene in a quiet forest, and finally feel liberated and joyful on a high mountain peak.**

The changing landscapes mirror their emotional journey.

### **8. The Grecian Guide – Introduce Local Guides**

Incorporate local guides into your scenes, much like a knowledgeable guide in ancient Greece.

These characters can provide insights into the new location, adding depth and authenticity.

They also serve as a natural way to introduce information about the setting.

**Example:** A character visiting Athens might be shown around by a local historian, who not only guides them through the city but also provides historical context and cultural anecdotes, enriching the scene and deepening the reader’s engagement.

### **9. The Nile Narrative – Streamline with a Continuous Plot**

Keep the plot flowing smoothly, like the Nile River.

Ensure that the movement between locations is driven by the plot, not just for the sake of change.

**Each location should serve a purpose in advancing the story.**

**Example:** In a mystery novel, the detective might follow clues from a bustling marketplace to a quiet suburb and then to a noisy train station. Each location change is a step closer to solving the mystery, keeping the plot cohesive.

### **10. The Kyoto Connection – Link Locations with a Character**

Use a character as the connecting thread, similar to a traveler exploring Kyoto’s varied districts.

This character’s journey and experiences in different settings can provide a consistent perspective and help the reader navigate the changes.

**Example:** A journalist character might travel from a war-torn region to a peaceful rural area and then to a hectic newsroom.

Their personal reactions and adaptations to each setting provide continuity and relatability for the reader.

### **11. The Roman Rhythm – Maintain a Consistent Pace**

**Ensure a consistent rhythm in your scene transitions, like the steady march of Roman legions.**

Avoid jarring the reader with abrupt changes.

A steady pace in shifting locations ensures a smooth reading experience.

**Example:** In an action-adventure story, balance fast-paced action sequences with slower, more introspective moments, regardless of the location. This maintains a rhythmic flow that keeps the reader engaged without overwhelming them.

### **12. The Antarctic Anchor – Use Objects or Symbols as Anchors**

Employ objects or symbols as anchors through the scene transitions, similar to how explorers use flags to mark their journey in Antarctica.

**This could be a recurring motif or a physical object that appears in each location.**

**Example:** A locket passed down through generations could appear in various settings – a bustling city, a quiet village, a lonely mountaintop. Its presence in each location creates a sense of continuity and connection.

### **13. The Caribbean Contrast – Use Contrasting Settings to Highlight Change**

Highlight change and development by contrasting settings, like the diverse islands of the Caribbean.

Use the stark differences between locations to underscore character growth or plot developments.

**Example:** A character might move from a chaotic urban environment to a serene rural setting. The contrast emphasizes their growth from a life of complexity to one of simplicity and peace.

### **14. The Silk Road Synthesis – Blend Cultural Elements**

Blend cultural elements from each location seamlessly, like the diverse influences along the Silk Road.

**This creates a rich, multicultural tapestry that enhances the narrative’s depth and authenticity.**

**Example:** In a historical novel, characters might travel from China through Central Asia to Europe, encountering different cultures, languages, and customs. The blend of these elements enriches the story and adds layers of complexity.

### **15. The Serengeti Strategy – Adapt Tone to Setting**

Adapt the tone of your writing to match each setting, similar to how animals adapt to different environments in the Serengeti.

This helps create an immersive experience for the reader, making each location feel authentic and alive.

**Example:** In a thriller, the tone might be tense and fast-paced in a crowded city scene, shift to cautious and suspenseful in a dark forest, and become relaxed and open in a peaceful seaside town.

### **16. The Amazonian Ambiance – Use Sensory Details**

**Engage all the senses to bring each location to life, like the rich sensory experience of the Amazon rainforest.**

Describe not just what the characters see, but also what they hear, smell, taste, and feel. This creates a vivid, immersive world for the reader.

**Example:** In a romantic novel, a character might experience the bustling sounds and spicy aromas of a street market, the soft touch and sweet scent of flowers in a garden, and the salty taste and cool breeze of the ocean.

### **17. The Trans-Siberian Transition – Use Movement as a Narrative Tool**

Finally, use the act of moving between locations as a narrative tool, akin to a journey on the Trans-Siberian Railway.

This movement can reflect the character’s inner journey or the progression of the plot.

**Example:** In an epic adventure, the protagonist’s journey from the steppes of Mongolia to the heart of Moscow can mirror their internal journey from naivety to wisdom. Each location change marks a stage in their personal growth and the unfolding of the story.

# Patrol simulator

https://youtu.be/nwYnZVttfaw?si=VNSoNAPK9HKH2pD7

With a patrol simulator, the real life behavior we are modelling is an indefinite total of branching paths. Essentially analog behavior.

However, good game design, and good gm prep depends on quantizing our branches. Furthermore, good story requires us to focus only on the quantized branches with the most significant impact on the gameplay, because weak differences between paths is represents an inefficient duplication of resources.

So how do we make quantized branches look indefinite?

One possible hypothesis to enable patrol simulator like behavior in a quantized environment would be to allow players to take the major branches, but then allow players to 'discover' their own versions of these branches that possess variant behaviors, such as more Stealth or better cover. We preserve the infinite possibility of open terrain to cross while saving the gm the need to manage excessive numbers of probable courses of action

Because players are looking for their own branches value, we can be sure that they are attempting to maximize their own interest in that region, so the gms job of making the arena interesting is partially unloaded

We can value these routes by using distance (or time) as a cost: players inserting better features that don't fit the poi take longer to travel.

In other words, the size of the region represents the cost of the effects. For example,  200 meter segments have 100 points of effect, and the second 100 points is an amount hidden behind increasing difficulty
