# Overview
The operational exchange has no initiative: both sides decide their actions and resolve them simultaneously.

During a tactical exchange, a team has three actions: major, minor, and passive.

Major actions are actions which have require a lot of attention or have a high probability of distracting the team. 

Minor actions are those which can be done in a distracted state.

Passive actions can be done at any time

The tactical exchange handles a team as one group.

Teams can prolong a tactical action across  multiple turns, such as movement. Bundled actions should be treated as a single challenge.

An **tactical scene** is the collection of all [exchanges of fire](/_AB13%20Low%20priority%20stuff/Chapter%205%20-%20Combat/_Master.md)[^2], pauses, [operational actions](/_AB13%20Low%20priority%20stuff/Chapter%205%20-%20Combat/_Master.md#Operational%20Actions)[^3], or tactical movements. These are collectively referred to as **tactical decisions**. tactical scenes begin when approaching the enemy.

Unlike tactical exchanges, tactical scenes do not have initiative rolls. Each group makes an tactical decision, then each turn is resolved simultaneously. Any group member interrupted by an exchange of fire during a non-combat related tactical action must give up their initiative check, even if they don't continue their tactical action. This does not apply to movement.

To simplify matters, a group may chain multiple tactical decisions together and then resolve it together.

tactical decisions range from 20 to 80 seconds in length, but can be simplified to 60. With an **tactical move** action, a group may move up to 10 times their best possible tactical speed. This move speed may be increased with an athletics or drive check as applicable.

The only time an attack can be made on the tactical scale is during an exchange of fire.

Information about the enemy group is limited during the tactical scene; characters only know where the enemy's center is, and are to be given no instructions on enemy intentions other than what they can intuit themselves, unless they make a reconnaissance  check (previously known as the keep watch check). 

The Withdraw tactical action is now classified as a [Stage]()[^4] I Rule. For Stage II rules, a group that wishes to disengage has to lose the opponent with obstructions or [intervisible](https://leaderbusiness.blogspot.com/2008/05/intervisibility-lines.html) concealment.

## starting encounter distances

The starting encounter distance is determined by a group spotting roll. The encounter starts at the distance of the margin of success, with each step of distance represented by the geometric scale (2^n).

The group spotting roll is determined by averaging the groups spot dice (all spot dice/the party size), rounded down. if this is too complicated, take the worst. 

The starting distance of a successful roll may be modified with the magnification rating of binoculars. If only a few people have binos, the chance their magnification is used is determined by chance (bino guy rolls 1d20,gm rolls 1d20. Whoever is higher wins)

At the discretion of the players, [[Roll mechanics#tradeoffs|side dice]] may be allocated for concealment, which penalized the opponents resulting [[Abiogenesis/_AB13 Low priority stuff/Chapter 3 - System/_Master#Observe-Orient-Decide-Act (OODA)|ooda]] roll.

The size of the opponent changes the distance by a number of steps

If neither succeeds, the encounter starts at either distance n=0 (25m) or never meet at the gms discretion.

## Encounter and sequence

Traditionally, RPGs bottle discrete encounters into a single location on a stage; for instance a fight won't leave one room.

however, the advent of the movie has broken this theatre limitation, allowing an encounter to occur over multiple locations. We can call this a sequence. These cuts are grouped together to convey a passage or montage of distance, time, or parallel efforts that are still considered one scene by cinematic standards.
## Scene/encounter

A situation located in one single point in space and time. This is the classic Gygaxian or stageplay encounter which will serve as a foundation for the new structure that telescopes from this. 

## Sequences, encounter chains, deep encounters
v1: Sequences are a chain of multiple scenes or encounters that spans long distances, multiple locations, time periods, or efforts. These sequences are connected together by 1 overarching story goal.
v2: A chain of related encounters distributed across a distance of time or place but sharing the same overarching conflict. 


Uncommon in the past, the encounter chain became a norm by the advance of weapon range to equal the range of vision, the graduation of the volume of weapon fire into the main source of fighting impact, and the increase of mobility provided by the car.[^3]

>7. **Globetrotting** – Typical in adventure stories where characters travel to multiple locations in search of something.

>[!Quote]
 A **map** is a [symbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol "Symbol") depiction emphasizing relationships between elements of some space, such as [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_body "Physical body"), [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region "Region"), or themes.

The infantry battle has a major problem where relevant-critical details can exist on layer 0 or layer -1, but we can't draw both fully and efficiently - we must constantly simplify out things such as microterrain or dead space, while taking them into account.

Playing at the tactical level requires terrain to be simplified with abstractions, called the level of detail.

Infantry battle maps have 3 layers. Each successive layer receives more player input

The most important level of detail is the base layer (0), followed by the landscape layer. Layer 0 handles most microfeatures as undepicted abstractions, unless necessary to a character.

**layer 1**: the landscape where the fight is located. The choice of layer 1 determines what layer 0 features can be picked and what mapwide behaviors exist in the map. layer 1 is always decided by the story. 

**layer 0**: the immediate features of the environment, the positive or negative space available that define the boundaries or spaces on the map. 

Layer zero starts with an initial subregion of (combat length) in distance with zero or more subregions attached to it. The combat value of a subregion depends on either random chance or the ability of a leader to locate good terrain.

Leaders can move across these different subregions, or find access to new subregions to move to work a tactics check, though at some point they will run out of options. Once a subregion is established it cannot be changed.

**layer -1**: the microfeatures that the players immediately use but are seldom drawn.

Characters may identify microfeatures to support their actions with a tactics check while supplies last and the enemy permits (ie: as they get progressively more pinned).
