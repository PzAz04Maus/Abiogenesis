# Deep encounters 
While all rpgs can reasonably handle the gunfight, the room crawl and the dungeon brawl, most do not scale well when imitating adventure and combat in societies with the car, the radio, or the rifle. If the distance between two antagonists can be as diverse as 1 to 1000 meters, the range of meaningful choices dramatically widens, but at a cost. To manage this effectively, scaling between the two extremes requires a robust, fluid game structure[^8].

[^8]:https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/15126/roleplaying-games/game-structures

## Chronos and Kairos

>Frequency is defined in physics as the number of periodic oscillations, vibrations, or waves occurring per unit of time. In military art, we can modify that definition by saying that frequency is the number of significant military events per unit time.[^1]

Our first problem is the flow of gameplay.  The symptoms of poor scaling can be seen in the moments where nothing meaningful is happening in character or out of character; dice may be rolled, people may be talking, but players are waiting for time to pass. For a game with resources as limited as on a tabletop, time wasted is a cost.

To refine our approach...

There are two ways we can define time. The first is our temporal norm, chronological time. Chronological time is defined as the number of equally spaced units of time. We can simplify this concept into a frequency with a fixed period of time between the ticks of our clock.[^2]

The other is backwards, defining the frequency of time by the importance of events in it. This is called Kairological time. For a tactical game, kairological time is the moments of opportunity that change the character of the battle.[^3].

## Changes

> When revolutionary changes occur in warfare, most of the time it is because there has been a change in frequency  at  some  level—a change in how fast  things happen.[^4]

This difference between chronological and kairological time presents several problems for fun tabletop gameplay. Firstly, simulationist-oriented players focus on chronos in order to enforce consistency, while narrative-oriented players focus on kairos in order to prioritize dramatic, meaningful choices. Because tabletop design norms have focused on the contradictions between the two, there appears to be an implicit bias against discussing the relationship between them, or in fact whether they can complement one another.

In fact, I personally believe that they are complementary enough to be considered two sides of the same coin. Nowhere is this understanding more conspicuous than a game trying to emulate modern industrialized adventure and combat.
