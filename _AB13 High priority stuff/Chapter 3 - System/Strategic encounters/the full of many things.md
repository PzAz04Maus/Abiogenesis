The sliding scale already exists even in fantasy games.
If the player tells me they want to go to a dungeon, and I say "absolutely. The next morning, you arrive", then we've converted a day of travel in the fiction to a 3-second time it takes me to say that sentence.

But if a player enters combat, takes a long time to decide what to do, then sometimes by the time the combat ends, we've converted 24 seconds of combat in the fiction to 2 hours of real time.

So, without any additional framework, that sliding scale is already existent and robust enough to handle that sort of distortion of time. 

Sometimes, modern activities might have timescales that the GM is less able to easily imagine - I can imagine swinging a sword or walking to a dungeon, but if you ask me to consider driving between Chicago and Indianapolis in the 1800s, I might not be able to come up with a good guess for what time that would take without looking some things up.

If this becomes important (for example, if there is an event happening in Indianapolis and we're not sure if we can make it), then yes, that can create some slowdown.

But largely, the slowdown has to do with modern questions of time having real, correct solutions that must be calculated. It has, in my view, very little to do with game mechanics.

In a modern game, if you want to chase a dragon using a Boeing 747, this provokes real questions about how fast a dragon flies. In a medieval game, the GM can much more easily get away with saying "you can't catch the dragon on horseback" - if the player asks "oh yeah, well how fast is my horse and how fast is the dragon", the GM doesn't have to answer very precisely. They can simply offer a deadpan look and say "dude, the dragon's faster than your horse. Use common sense here."
