# Concept 1: Sequences and Encounters
## General facts

## starting encounter distances

The starting encounter distance is determined by a group spotting roll. The encounter starts at the distance of the margin of success, with each step of distance represented by the geometric scale (2^n).

The group spotting roll is determined by averaging the groups spot dice (all spot dice/the party size), rounded down. if this is too complicated, take the worst. 

The starting distance of a successful roll may be modified with the magnification rating of binoculars. If only a few people have binos, the chance their magnification is used is determined by chance (bino guy rolls 1d20,gm rolls 1d20. Whoever is higher wins)

At the discretion of the players, side dice may be allocated for concealment, which penalized the opponents resulting ooda roll.

The size of the opponent changes the distance by a number of steps

If neither succeeds, the encounter starts at either distance n=0 (25m) or never meet at the gms discretion. 

By default

## Encounter and sequence

Traditionally, RPGs bottle discrete encounters into a single location on a stage; for instance a fight won't leave one room.

however, the advent of the movie has broken this theatre limitation, allowing an encounter to occur over multiple locations. We can call this a sequence. These cuts are grouped together to convey a passage or montage of distance, time, or parallel efforts that are still considered one scene by cinematic standards.
## Scene/encounter

A situation located in one single point in space and time. This is the classic Gygaxian or stageplay encounter which will serve as a foundation for the new structure that telescopes from this. 

## Sequences, encounter chains, deep encounters
v1: Sequences are a chain of multiple scenes or encounters that spans long distances, multiple locations, time periods, or efforts. These sequences are connected together by 1 overarching story goal.
v2: A chain of related encounters distributed across a distance of time or place but sharing the same overarching conflict. 


Uncommon in the past, the encounter chain became a norm by the advance of weapon range to equal the range of vision, the graduation of the volume of weapon fire into the main source of fighting impact, and the increase of mobility provided by the car.[^3]

Sequences are important for modern games because the velocity people can act on in a brief amount of time has increased dramatically. Cars operate at highway speeds, Firearms have a muzzle velocity measured in thousands of feet per second, telecommunications can operate at the speed of light. There are many situations where important things occur on this scale in the modern thriller which cannot be efficiently work in a bottled scene. For example, rules for fuel or ammunition may be quite useful story elements, but are inappropriate to be handled on a per room basis.

>The montage is used to communicate a big chunk of story in a short span of time, with the goal of still being able to allow the reader and audience to experience the informational and emotional elements of that chunk of story. Or it is used to communicate multiple reactions or experiences of multiple characters in multiple locations at the same time.

Traditional theatre examples of sequences

> 1. **The Chase Sequence** – This is action-packed, moving rapidly from one locale to another as characters pursue or escape.
>2. **The Montage** – Here, you might show snippets of different places to indicate the passage of time or a character’s emotional journey.
>3. **Parallel Action** – This type cuts between different locations where events unfold simultaneously, often building suspense.
>4. **Flashbacks/Forwards** – These scenes jump in time, taking the reader from the present to the past or future, often revealing crucial plot points.
>5. **Cross-Cultural Juxtaposition** – Scenes that move between vastly different cultural settings, highlighting contrasts and similarities.
>6. **Dream Sequences** – These can transport the reader to various surreal or symbolic locations, reflecting the character’s inner psyche.
>7. **Global Quests** – Typical in adventure stories where characters travel to multiple locations in search of something.

# Patrol simulator

https://youtu.be/nwYnZVttfaw?si=VNSoNAPK9HKH2pD7

With a patrol simulator, the real life behavior we are modelling is an indefinite total of branching paths. Essentially analog behavior.

However, good game design, and good gm prep depends on quantizing our branches. Furthermore, good story requires us to focus only on the quantized branches with the most significant impact on the gameplay, because weak differences between paths is represents an inefficient duplication of resources.

So how do we make quantized branches look indefinite?

One possible hypothesis to enable patrol simulator like behavior in a quantized environment would be to allow players to take the major branches, but then allow players to 'discover' their own versions of these branches that possess variant behaviors, such as more Stealth or better cover. We preserve the infinite possibility of open terrain to cross while saving the gm the need to manage excessive numbers of probable courses of action

Because players are looking for their own branches value, we can be sure that they are attempting to maximize their own interest in that region, so the gms job of making the arena interesting is partially unloaded

We can value these routes by using distance (or time) as a cost: players inserting better features that don't fit the poi take longer to travel.

In other words, the size of the region represents the cost of the effects. For example,  200 meter segments have 100 points of effect, and the second 100 points is an amount hidden behind increasing difficulty
